"In Plato's Cave" On Photography

I use photography as a way to preserve and deepen human connections. I have so many blurry memories as a child that I wish I could've captured to be able to really see a clear picture. Like Susan Sontag mentioned, “People robbed of their past seem to make the most fervent picture takers, at home and abroad.” With the minimal photos I do have, I cherish them more and more as time goes by and the images start aging. For example, I have a memory of my father and me dancing, and he’s throwing me in the air; it is one of my favorite childhood memories ever. I unfortunately don't have any images of that time of my life, but I do have a photo of him and me dancing a couple of years later. If I hadn’t seen that image, I probably would've forgotten those special moments, and considering I hadn't seen him in years, the memories I had in my head and minimal physical pictures were the only things I had left to hold on to. I refused to forget those moments and the culture I grew up in. Those photographs influenced me a lot growing up. It would trigger memories of songs I would hear at family parties, cultural dances, and foods. Doing research and rediscovering so much of my childhood I had completely forgotten about helped me keep and preserve my connections with family members despite our distances. Many people from older generations did not have the privilege of smartphones, who wish they had more images from their younger lives. “Photographs actively promote nostalgia.” Instead, they only have a glimpse of images in their head for them to daydream about.

The Gaurdian "With 'AI slop'"

In The Guardian article “With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster,” Nesrine Malik argues that the internet is becoming overwhelmed by low-quality AI-generated content that blurs the line between truth and fiction. The reading allowed me to reflect on how much social media has changed in such a short amount of time. Specifically, from the time I was entering the first year of high school, apps like Snapchat and Instagram had just experienced (in my opinion) their big boom in popularity. Fake pics were, for the most part, very easily spotted, especially since Photoshop and editing apps weren’t as advanced. Until now, where every photo and video is being heavily dissected for AI. Platforms that were once meant for human creativity and communication now often feel artificial, repetitive, and manipulative. Malik’s discussion of “AI slop” was especially unsettling because many of the examples she describes are already common online: fake emotional videos, unrealistic AI-generated images, and misleading political content designed to gain attention and engagement.  One of the most important points the article makes is that this content is not just harmless entertainment. Malik explained how social media companies benefit financially from emotionally charged AI material because it keeps users scrolling and interacting, furthering the addiction of “doom scrolling”. This made me think about how algorithms reward quantity and outrage over quality and truth, and how I’ve been personally affected by it. Sometimes I’ll spend hours glued to TikTok and Instagram, knowing in the back of my mind I have work to get done. Instead of encouraging meaningful conversations or creativity between humans, platforms prioritize whatever gets the most clicks. Some call it “clickbait” or even “rage bait” when using platforms to actively upset people into arguments online just for attention. The system has become extremely greedy. As a result, users are constantly exposed to exaggerated or fake content, slowly shaping how they see reality. I found this argument convincing because it connects technology to larger social problems such as misinformation, political manipulation, and the decline of trust online. A concern that’s been talked about in media classes since the rise of social media, and now we are currently living in the reality that the warnings were trying to protect us from.  The article also made me think about how difficult it is becoming to identify what is real. AI-generated images and videos are becoming increasingly convincing, and many people may lack the media literacy skills to question what they see. Malik points out that older adults and less digitally experienced users are especially vulnerable to being misled. I think this is important because social media now plays a major role in shaping public opinion. If people can no longer trust visual evidence, society risks becoming more divided and confused. The article suggests that the problem is not just fake content itself, but the feeling of disorientation it creates. At the same time, the reading made me consider the future of creativity and human expression online. AI-generated content is cheap and fast, which makes it attractive to companies. However, it lacks a genuine human perspective and emotion. I agree with Malik’s concern that constant exposure to artificial content could reduce appreciation for real art, journalism, and storytelling. We already see this happening with people falling emotionally in love and becoming dependent on AI platforms like ChatGPT. Overall, this article was both insightful and disturbing because it highlights how technology can reshape reality in ways many people do not yet fully notice. It left me thinking more critically about the media I consume every day and about the responsibilities of both users and technology companies to protect truthful and meaningful communication online. 

Al Jazeera "Motherhood in Crisis"

In the Al Jazeera article “Motherhood in Crisis,” the stories of women in Sierra Leone reveal how dangerous pregnancy and childbirth can become when healthcare systems are weak and social inequality is widespread. The reading was emotional, disturbing, and relatable because it portrayed motherhood not as a joyful experience but as one filled with fear, hardship, and survival. This is often not the case when women are given a platform to discuss motherhood. There is always this undertone and pressure on women to say motherhood is nothing but rainbows and sunshine, when in reality it can be just as disturbing and hard as any other difficulties. Motherhood is not just about giving birth; you are now responsible for an entire human being who will eventually be a part of society one day. That pressure is immense. Through the personal stories of women like Memunatu, Heela, Kadiatu, and Tetteh, the article humanizes statistics on maternal mortality. It helps readers understand the emotional and physical consequences of poverty and limited access to healthcare.  The reading points out how geography and poverty directly affected the women’s chances of survival. Many women in rural areas had to travel long distances while pregnant to receive medical care, and even then, hospitals often lacked proper resources. I thought of the many immigrant women who crossed through the desert to arrive in the USA in hopes of a better healthcare system, due to their country not providing that for them. In wealthier countries, pregnancy is usually treated as a manageable medical condition with consistent support. However, in Sierra Leone and America, it is presented as a major risk. The article emphasizes that these dangers are not natural or unavoidable; they stem from political and economic conditions that leave healthcare systems underfunded. It also explores how motherhood intersects with gender inequality. Several of the women faced pressure, abandonment, or violence because of pregnancy. Their stories demonstrate how women often carry the burden of family survival while receiving little protection or support themselves. I found Heela’s story especially tragic because it showed how a teenage pregnancy disrupted her education and future opportunities, an experience many young girls go through, including some people I personally know. Instead of being supported, she lost control over her future because of circumstances tied to poverty and social expectations. This reflects a larger issue in many societies where girls’ education and independence are sacrificed once motherhood becomes involved. Another important part of the article is its use of the graphic novel format. The illustrations make the women’s experiences feel more personal and emotional than a standard news report might. Visual storytelling creates empathy and allows readers to connect more deeply with the fear, grief, and exhaustion the women experience. I think this format is effective because it combines journalism and art, making the stories more memorable and accessible. Rather than overwhelming readers with statistics alone that can often come off as “boring”, because motherhood is not about numbers, it is about the reality of life and the beginning of many babies’ lives. The article focuses on individual human experiences. Overall, “Motherhood in Crisis” highlights how motherhood can become life-threatening when governments fail to provide proper healthcare and social support. The article challenged me to think more critically about global inequality and women’s rights. It also reminded me that issues like maternal healthcare are not just medical problems, but social and political ones that reflect how societies value women’s lives.
